Global Significance of Serpentine on Mars

Author: A. Emran (3223 JPL Postdoctoral Fellow)
J. D. Tarnas (Blue); and K. M. Stack (3223)
INTRODUCTION
  • Serpentine is an astrobiologically compelling mineral on Mars. H2 and CH4 (in presence of CO2) are produced during its production via the oxidation of olivine and pyroxene (mafic and ultra-mafic rocks) by water [1]
  • Chemolithotrophic microbial communities rely on H2 and CH4 nutrient sources [2]
  • Serpentine has characteristics absorption bands at 1.38, 2.12, 2.32, and 2.52 µm at near infrared wavelengths [3]
  • 2.12 µm is the most characteristic band for a unique identification (Fig. 1)
  • Identifying serpentine on Mars using I/F data from the Compact Reconnaissance Imaging Spectrometer for Mars (CRISM) is challenging due to the presence of an artifact in the images [4]
  • Requires a validation of 2.12 µm band in radiance data — called radiance ratio technique [4] (Fig. 2)

OBJECTIVE
  • Create an updated global map of serpentine for Mars using CRISM images
  • using factor analysis target transform method [6]
  • validated with both I/F and radiance data
  • Geologic context and mineral assemblage associated with serpentine
  • Timing and amount (approx.) of H2 produced via serpentinization on Mars
  • Extend of serpentinization on the surface and subsurface
METHODOLOGY
  • A total of 15,760 CRISM Targeted Reduced Data Record (TRDR) images
  • Uses all TRDR records viz. 7885 Full Resolution Targeted (FRT), 2785 Half Resolution Long (HRL), 3738 Full Resolution Short (FRS), 1352 and Half Resolution Short (HRS)
  • Covers 90° N to 90° S latitudes (~1.2% of Martian surface)
  • Atmospherically and photometrically corrected using the standard CRISM processing pipeline including volcano-scan calibration [7]
  • Apply dynamic aperture factor analysis/target transformation (DAFA/TT) [6] to the calibrated CRISM images
  • I/F and radiance ratio spectra from the DAFA/TT detected pixels
  • Detections are assigned to confidence values– 1 (low/weak) to 5 (high/best)
  • Geologic context using CTX and THEMIS-IR daytime mosaics [8-9]
  • Age of serpentine host unit [10] and associated minerals from CRISM
  • Influence of dust in surface detection using dust cover index map [11]
  • Amount of H2 produced using the rates from analogue rocks [12]

## Key findings ##
  • Detected in 43 CRISM images, with detections in 32 of the images representing “new” or previously unrecognized detections
  • Occurrence between 40°N to 40°S latitudes, and no detections between 90°E to 180°E longitudes
  • Formation in a wide variety of landforms (craters, valleys, and plains)
  • 31 out of 43 detected images on Noachian-aged surface units
  • Younger detections are associated with impact craters— possibility that serpentine formed earlier at depth, and was subsequently excavated
  • Serpentine detections correspond with lower average dust cover
  • Often occurs near olivine, low-Ca pyroxene, clays, and carbonate minerals
  • Mg-rich serpentine detections cover a total surface area of ~1.46 km²
  • Estimated max. production (~10 km) of 5.11 × 10¹¹ and 4.85 × 10¹³ mol H₂

DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION
  • Only 11 CRISM images previously thought to contain serpentine [13] could be validated for the presence of the mineral using the radiance ratio technique
  • Most serpentine deposits identified here occur in Noachian-aged terrains implying that serpentinization occurred predominantly early in Mars’ history
  • Although it occurs in a wide variety of landscapes, the detection of serpentine in craters raises the possibility that impact-induced hydrothermal serpentinization was an important process for serpentine formation
  • Although serpentinization is favored thermodynamically in mafic/ultramafic rocks on the surface and in the subsurface [14-15], Mg-serpentine deposits was found to be generally rare and of relatively limited areal extent
  • Reasons for the apparent paucity of serpentine on Mars could include:
    a). Limitations of orbiter sensor capability and the impact of dust and/or sub-pixel mixing
    b). Inability to uniquely detect Fe-rich serpentine on Mars [16]
    c). CRISM data covers only ~1.2% of the Martian surface
    d). Serpentine may once have formed extensively on early Mars, but has subsequently weathered and altered to clay minerals and carbonates
    e). Serpentinization occurred primarily in the subsurface and is observable only when excavated by impact cratering or other tectonic processes
