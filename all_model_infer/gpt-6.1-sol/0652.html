Victims frequently report immobility during
rape and sexual assault, often using the
term ‘freezing’. Neuroscientific evidence
suggests fear and threat can block cortical
neural circuits for action control, leading to
involuntary immobility. Defence arguments
that blame victims for freezing are thus
inappropriate and unjust.

Rape and sexual assault (collectively, RSA) are distinctive types of
aggressive and criminal human behaviour that are overwhelmingly
committed by men and directed at women. Thirty per cent of women
have experienced RSA in their lifetime. The burdens of RSA on individ-
uals and whole societies are extensive, but the subject is often hidden. For
example, in England and Wales the police recorded over 70,000 rapes
in 2021–2022 but only 3% led to a charge. Neuroscientific contributions
to public debate over RSA have been limited.

Consent and rape myths

Legal definitions of RSA are based on the absence of consent. However,
establishing consent or lack thereof is challenging. Victims’ reports of
non-consent are often questioned in court. Legal actors in RSA cases
are susceptible to stereotypes (referred to here as ‘rape myths’) about
how a ‘real’ victim would behave.

One common rape myth1 involves perpetrator claims that they
had assumed consent from the absence of clear attempts by the victim
to resist: ‘why didn’t she struggle?’ Although struggle and physical force
is not part of legal definitions of RSA in many countries, rape myths
such as this continue to influence the thinking of jurors, lawyers and
judges, and of wider society. Box 1 gives indicative examples of these
misogynistic arguments, which seek to transfer the blame for RSA from
the perpetrator to the victim.

The ‘why didn’t she struggle?’ argument is based on a cognitive
model of intentional action that underlies all criminal law. Healthy adult
humans are assumed to have voluntary control over their actions and
to carry out their actions intentionally. Mens rea (conscious intention)
makes agents responsible for their actions and their consequences. Philo-
sophical and legal arguments hold that people are not responsible for
omitting to act — that not acting implies choice, in the same way
that acting is intentional. In the case of RSA, this argument incorrectly
assumes that the victim could have resisted or fled the attacker, yet she
intentionally decided not to do so.

The neuroscience of involuntary immobility

We argue that the assumption that [illegible] choose immo-
bility over resistance is neuroscientifically incorrect. We suggest that
RSA victims may remain immobile because of an involuntary neural
response to threat2 that blocks the brain circuitry that provide voluntary
control over body movement.

While there was evidence of the victim saying ‘no’ on several
occasions, there was no evidence that she ever resisted because it would
have been futile. When asked why she froze, [she] answered “I don’t

Further, legal discussions of the involuntary immobility response
may involve an inappropriate double standard. The law has long rec-
ognized ‘loss of control’ defences and can accord diminished respon-
sibility in specific situations in which evidence suggests that actions
are made outside of voluntary control. These include some medical
conditions such as sleep disorders, but also extreme situations of
coercive control, and emotional triggering.

To be consistent, legal systems should likewise recognize that the
omission of action may also sometimes be involuntary. An involuntary
immobility response may prevent a victim of RSA from responding
by escaping or deterring the aggressor. This does not imply that the
victim ought to make such actions: obligations and responsibilities
in RSA lie with the aggressor, and not with the victim. Some legal
understanding of neuroscience evidence regarding involuntary
immobility during RSA could prevent inappropriate victim-blaming,
and potentially draw wider societal attention to the crucial impor-
tance of active consent. Clearly, it is also possible to be voluntarily
immobile without actively consenting, but our aim here is to draw
attention to the frequency, mechanism and legal implications of
involuntary immobility.
