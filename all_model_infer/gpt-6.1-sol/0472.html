Astronomers call supermassive black
holes and their surrounding disks
“Active Galactic Nuclei” or “AGN”

Black holes emit no light, but
the glow from the bright
material swirling around them
can be seen by telescopes.

Why Do Active Black Holes Look Different?

[Diagram: face-on view of the dusty doughnut]

In some galaxies the
black hole regions are
incredibly bright, while in
other galaxies they
appear much darker.

A “unified model”
suggests the angle at
which we view them can
explain the differences.

[Diagram: edge-on view of the dusty doughnut]

Seen face-on, we detect the light from the
hot material surrounding the black hole.

When viewed through the edge of the dusty
doughnut, the black hole is hidden.

Galaxies are oriented randomly in the sky so disks
in their centers should be oriented randomly as well.

Thus we expect to see a random mix of exposed and
hidden black holes everywhere we look.

WISE Observations Challenge “Unified Model”

[Illustration: WISE spacecraft above Earth]

Observations with NASA’s Wide-field Infrared Survey Explorer reveal that hidden black holes tend to be more clumped together than exposed ones.

This violates the “unified model,” which predicts we should see the same random distribution of doughnuts regardless of how clumped the objects are.

[Illustration: strongly clumped galaxies with purple dark-matter halos]

Strongly clumped galaxies are likely to have larger halos of dark matter (shown artistically here in purple), which might provide a clue about what else besides dust clouds could be hiding the black holes.
