Neuroscience evidence counters a rape myth

Ebani Dhawan & Patrick Haggard
✓ Check for updates

Victims frequently report immobility during rape and sexual assault, often using the term 'freezing'. Neuroscientific evidence suggests fear and threat can block cortical neural circuits for action control, leading to involuntary immobility. Defence arguments that blame victims for freezing are thus inappropriate and unjust.

Rape and sexual assault (collective) behaviour (RSA) are distinctive types of aggressive and criminal behaviour that are overwhelmingly experienced by men and directed at women. Thirty per cent of women have experienced RSA in their lifetime. The burdens of RSA on individuals and wider societies are extensive, and subjects of often hidden. For example, only 15% of victims report RSA to police in England and Wales in 2022, but only 3% led to change. Neuroscientific contributions to public debate over RSA have been limited.

Consent and rape myths

Legal definitions of RSA are based on the challenge of consent. However, establishing consent or lack thereof is challenging. Victims' reports of non-consent are often questioned in court. Legal actors in RSA cases use susceptible stereotypes (referred here as 'rape myths') about how victims should behave during an assault.

One common rape myth involves a perpetrator claiming that he had assumed consent from the absence of clear attempts by the victim to resist: why didn't she struggle? Although stakeholders and violence play important roles in RSA, the victim's lack of physical resistance has continued to influence the thinking of juries, lawyers and judges, and of wider society. Box 1 gives indicative examples of these misogynistic arguments, which seek to transfer the blame for RSA from the perpetrator to the victim: why didn't she struggle?

Why didn't she struggle? argument is based on a cognitive model of intentional action that undergirds all criminal law. Healthy adult humans are assumed to have voluntary control over their actions to carry out their actions intentionally. Men's sex (conscious intention) and actus reus (the act) are both required elements of a crime. Sophisticated and legal arguments hold that one may also be responsible for omitting to act – not acting that may be intentional in the same way as acting is intentional. In the case of RSA, this argument incorrectly assumes that the victim's failure to resist was intended; yet the intentionality decided not to do so.

The neuroscience of involuntary immobility

We argue that the assumption that victims intentionally choose immobility during RSA is inconsistent with neuroscientific evidence. We suggest that RSA victims may remain immobile because of an involuntary neural response to threat that blocks the brain circuits that provide voluntary control over body movement.

Legal discussions of the involuntary immobility response may involve an inappropriate double standard. The law has long recognized that some situations can render a person incapable of making choices outside of specific statutory contexts. Such evidence suggests that medical conditions such as sleep disorders, but also extreme situations of coercive control, and emotional triggering likewise recognize that the omission of action may also sometimes be involuntary. An involuntary immobility response may prevent a victim of RSA from responding in escaping or deterring the aggressor. This does not imply that the victim is consenting to the assault, or cooperating with the victim. Improved legal understanding of neuroscientific evidence regarding involuntary immobility during RSA could prevent inappropriate victim-blaming, and potentially draw societal attention to the crucial importance of distinguishing between voluntary choice and involuntary immobility without actively consenting, but our aim here is to draw attention to the frequency, mechanism and legal implications of involuntary immobility.
