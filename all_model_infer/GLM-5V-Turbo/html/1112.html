Involuntary action and the law

The interpretation of IRSA as an involuntary threat-induced block of volition has implications for both legal and philosophical concepts of voluntariness, autonomy and consent. Voluntary action depends on the functioning of specific brain circuits that are centred on the frontal lobes and also on the nonengagement of other brain circuits that are specialized for processing threats. These specialized circuits include specialized circuits that also involve subcortical structures3.

Legal systems provide normative principles for investigating, explaining and judging human voluntary actions4. Legal process requires that actions be attributed to agents who performed them. Conclusion

We argue that IRSA is an evolutionarily conserved motor response that is characterized by a lack of normal voluntary motor control, with preservation of some postural tone. We suggest that it depends on subsequent memory effects. We hypothesize that IRSA emerges when the aggressor's behaviour activates the brain threat defence circuitry of the victim, leading to the inhibition of neural pathways that underlie voluntary action.

This interaction between neural circuits for threat processing and for voluntary action remains a scientific hypothesis, as we can have only indirect evidence regarding patterns of neural activity during IRSA.
