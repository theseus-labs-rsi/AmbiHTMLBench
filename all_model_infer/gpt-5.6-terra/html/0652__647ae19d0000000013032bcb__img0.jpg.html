Rape and sexual assault (collectively, RSA) are distinctive types of aggressive and criminal human behaviour that are overwhelmingly committed by men and directed at women. Thirty per cent of women have experienced RSA in their lifetime. The burdens of RSA on individuals and whole societies are extensive, but the subject of victim hoods. For example, in England and Wales the police recorded over 70,000 rapes in 2021–2022 but only 3% led to a charge. Neuroscientific contributions to public debate over RSA have been limited.

Consent and rape myths

Legal definitions of RSA are based on the absence of consent. However, establishing consent or lack thereof is challenging. Victims’ reports of non-consent are often questioned in court. Legal actors in RSA cases have sought to stereotype ‘freezer’ there as ‘rape myths’ about how a ‘real’ victim would behave.

One common rape myth involves a perpetrator claiming that he had assumed consent from the absence of clear attempts by the victim to resist: ‘why didn’t she struggle?’ Although struggle and violence play no part in formal legal definitions of RSA in many countries, rape myths such as this continue to influence the thinking of jurors, lawyers and judges, and of wider society. Box 1 gives indicative examples of these misogynistic arguments, which seek to transfer the blame for RSA from the perpetrator to the victim.

The ‘why didn’t she struggle?’ argument is based on a cognitive model of intentional action that underlies all criminal law. Healthy adult humans are assumed to have voluntary control over their actions and to carry out their actions intentionally. Mens rea (conscious intention) makes agents responsible for their actions and consequences. Philosophical and legal arguments hold that one may also be responsible for omitting to act — that not acting may be intentional in the same way as that acting is intentional. In the case of RSA, this argument incorrectly assumes that the victim could have resisted or fled the attacker, yet he intentionally decided not to do so.

The neuroscience of involuntary immobility

We argue that the assumption that victims intentionally choose immobility over resistance is neuroscientifically incorrect. We suggest that RSA victims may remain immobile because of an involuntary neural response to threat that blocks the brain circuits that provide voluntary control over body movement.

Further, legal discussions of the involuntary immobility response may involve an inappropriate double standard. The law has long recognized ‘loss of control’ defences and can accord diminished responsibility in specific situations in which evidence suggests that actions are made outside of voluntary control. These include some medical conditions such as sleep disorders, but also extreme situations of coercive control, and emotional triggering.

To be consistent, legal systems should likewise recognize that the omission of action may also sometimes be involuntary. An involuntary immobility response may prevent a victim of RSA from responding by escaping or deterring the aggressor. This does not imply that the victim ought to make such actions: obligations and responsibilities in RSA lie with the aggressor, and not with the victim. Improved legal understanding of neuroscientific evidence regarding involuntary immobility during RSA could prevent inappropriate victim-blaming, and potentially draw wider societal attention to the crucial importance of active consent. Clearly, it is also possible to be voluntarily immobile without actively consenting, but our aim here is to draw attention to the frequency, mechanism and legal implications of involuntary immobility.
