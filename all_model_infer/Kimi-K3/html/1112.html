In cats, ventral reticulospinal axons with cell bodies in the gigantocellular nucleus synapse onto inhibitory spinal interneurons, suggesting a candidate mechanism for inhibiting voluntary action. Inhibitory circuits also exist within the human brainstem related to defensive responses, but these have not been studied in the context of threat processing. Second, we suggest that two forms of IRSA might exist: one in which muscle tone is preserved (similar to the classical concept of tonic immobility) and a hypotonic ‘floppy’ form of immobility. Further research on humans is required to describe the presentation, aetiology and sequelae of these two behaviours, including their implications for victim rehabilitation and for legal outcomes. Importantly, both forms of IRSA would constitute involuntary responses to extreme threat and both would involve the suspension of normal voluntary action control.

Involuntary action and the law

Our interpretation of IRSA as an involuntary, threat-induced block of normal voluntary action control raises profound questions about volition, autonomy and consent. Voluntary action depends on the functioning of specific brain circuits that are centred on the frontal lobes and also on the nonengagement of other brain circuits that are specialized for defensive responses. IRSA involves preserved specialized circuits that also involve subcortical structures.

Legal systems provide normative principles for investigating, explaining and judging human voluntary actions1. Legal process distinguishes voluntary actions from involuntary behaviours, and explains both actions and omissions by reference to the intentions of agents. Individuals are held responsible for their actions and omissions when these are intentional and voluntary, but responsibility is qualified or reduced when actions and omissions are unintentional — and individuals are typically not held responsible for actions that are involuntary. Thus, legal concepts of responsibility inevitably implicate the neurophysiological question of the role of cortical voluntary action circuits in behaviour.

We suggest that given the neural mechanisms that underlie responses to severe threat, IRSA can be considered as an involuntary omission of voluntary action. As such, legal ‘why’ questions regarding IRSA need to be answered with reference to involuntary actions and omissions. Legal process should recognize this fact and ensure it is consistently applied.

We highlight a second issue for legal process surrounding IRSA. Legal actors often assume that accounts by victims of immobility during IRSA are conditioned and lack conventional explanatory terms (Box 1). The victim may have difficulty answering ‘why’ questions. Defence lawyers often exploit this fact and draw attention to a victim’s inability to explain and justify their behaviour, using this as a route to straightforward victim-blaming, diverting the attention of the court away from the aggressive behaviour of the assailant towards the purportedly strange behaviour of the victim. In fact, recent neuroscientific evidence (see Box 1) also helps explain why victims often have difficulty explaining their own behaviour. Victims’ accounts of IRSA are likely to share the fragmented, incoherent quality that is characteristic of traumatic memories in general.

In addition, memories of IRSA refer to an unprecedented experience — namely, losing voluntary control over one’s own body. Voluntary agency is the chief backdrop to mental life in healthy adults, and so a sudden loss of ability to act in accordance with one’s wishes is likely to constitute a surprising and disturbing event for which one has no neuroscientific evidence from a range of conditions confirms both the disorganization of traumatic memory2 and the bizarreness of losing

volitional control3 that are also present in IRSA. The law already recognizes as evidential guidelines that trauma may affect the ability to remember and explain events, including one’s own behaviour — but this point seems often to be ignored in legal discussions of IRSA.

Conclusion

We argue that IRSA is an evolutionarily conserved involuntary response that is characterized by lack of normal voluntary motor control, with further preserved capacity for nonvoluntary threat responses and subsequent memory effects. We hypothesize that IRSA emerges when the aggressor’s behaviours activate the brain threat-defence circuitry of the victim, leading to the inhibition of neural pathways that underlie voluntary action.

This interaction between neural circuits for threat processing and for voluntary action remains a scientific hypothesis, as we can have only indirect evidence regarding patterns of neural activity during IRSA. However, convergent evidence from victims’ accounts and testimony and evidence from studies of defence circuitry in animals. This evidence, although indirect, is substantial and convergent. Ethical and moral hazards surround almost all research in this area, which makes direct experimental work difficult. Studies could still, in our view, contribute to public debate regarding IRSA. For example, mechanistic understanding of IRSA may help to counteract rape myths in the media and in the courtroom. In addition, the involuntary inhibition of voluntary action pathways may contribute to improving understanding of the facts about IRSA crimes, the societal wrongs of gender violence, and the realities of victims’ experience and suffering.

Neuroscientists can also make important contributions to justice in this area. For example, a recent intervention study has shown that educating police officers to understand the involuntary neural mechanisms of victim responses to threat led to more accurate and scientifically informed training for officers improved victims’ willingness to continue with legal proceedings4 and such training could potentially improve legal outcomes and justice. Finally, increased awareness of IRSA among lawyers and the public could help to reduce victim-blaming, including self-blaming and inappropriate feelings of guilt.

A recent Spanish law explicitly requires that consent must be freely and clearly expressed by a person’s actions. This progressive and enlightened legislative change recognizes that a lack of resistance could ever be interpreted as consent. Arguments and attitudes that imply that immobility might be misinterpreted as consent are neuroscientifically mistaken, and unjustly blame victims. Neuroscience may have a role in helping legal systems and wider society to guard against such rape myths.
