In cats, ventral reticulospinal axons with cell bodies in the gigantocellular nucleus synapse onto inhibitory spinal interneurons, suggesting a candidate mechanism for involuntary action in cats. Inhibitory circuits also exist within the human brainstem itself, but these have not been studied in the context of threat processing.

Second, we suggest that two forms of fRSA might exist: one in which muscle tone is preserved (similar to the classical concept of tonic immobility) and a hypotonic 'floppy' form of immobility. Further research is required to determine whether these two forms of fRSA follow a sequence of these two behaviours, including their implications for victim rehabilitation and for legal outcomes. Importantly, both forms of fRSA would constitute involuntary responses to extreme threat and both would involve the suspension of normal voluntary action control.

Involuntary action and the law

Our interpretation of fRSA as an involuntary, threat-induced block of normal voluntary action control raises profound questions about how involuntary action and criminal responsibility are expressed. The functioning of specific brain circuits that are centred on the frontal lobes and also on the nonengagement of other brain circuits that are specialized for action control suggests that victims with preserved specialized circuits that also involve subcortical structures.

Legal systems provide normative principles for investigating, explaining and judging human behaviour. The criminal law provides often poses 'why' questions about human behaviour and explains both actions and omissions by reference to the intentions of agents. Involuntary actions happen because the intentions of agents are intentional and voluntary, but responsibility is qualified or reduced when actions and omissions are unintentional — and individuals are typically not held responsible for actions that are involuntary. Thus, legal concepts of responsibility inevitably implicate the neurobiological mechanisms of the role of cortical voluntary action circuits in control of behaviour.

We suggest that given the neural mechanisms that underlie involuntary action during threat, the concept of fRSA involving the omission of voluntary action. As such, legal 'why' questions regarding fRSA would not have the same force as for intentional actions or omissions. The legal process should recognize this fact and ensure it is consistently applied.

We highlight a second issue for legal process surrounding fRSA. Legal systems assume that individuals can explain their actions. However, fRSA often disjointed and lack conventional explanatory terms (Box 1). The victim may have difficulty answering 'why' questions. Defence lawyers may find that the victim's attention to a victim's inability to articulate and justify their behaviour during RSA. This appears to be straightforward victim-blaming, diverting the attention of the court away from the aggressive behaviour of the assailant towards the purportedly strange behaviour of the victim. In fact, recent neuroscientific advances can also help in understanding why victims often have difficulty explaining their own behaviour. The accounts of fRSA are likely to share the fragmented, incoherent quality that is characteristic of traumatic memories in general12.

In addition, accounts of fRSA refer to an unprecedented experience — namely, losing voluntary control over one's own body. Voluntary agency is the chief way to mental life in healthy adults, and the sudden inability to act according to one's wishes is likely to seem strange and inexplicable even in the absence of trauma. Clinical neuroscience research on the range of symptoms following the disorganization of traumatic memory13 and the bizarreness of losing

volitional control14 that are also present in fRSA. The law already recognizes in evidential guidelines that trauma may affect the ability to recall events and explain events, including one's own behaviour — but this point seems rarely to be ignored in legal discussions of fRSA.

Conclusion

We argue that fRSA is an evolutionarily conserved immobility response that is characterized by lack of normal voluntary motor control, with a range of autonomic and physiological changes that may persist into subsequent memory effects. We hypothesize that fRSA emerges when the aggressor's behaviours activate the brain threat-defence circuitry in the victim, leading to the inhibition of neural pathways that underlie the victim's voluntary action control.

This interpretation between neural circuits of threat processing and of voluntary action remains a scientific hypothesis, as we can have only indirect evidence regarding patterns of neural activity during fRSA. Instead, our argument relies on indirect evidence such as victim reports and evidence from animal models of threat-induced immobility. This evidence, although indirect, is substantial and convergent. Ethical and moral hazards surround almost all research in this area, which is why we believe that more research is needed to address the legal and societal issues raised by fRSA. However, neuroscience should still, in our view, contribute to public debate regarding fRSA. Neuroscientific mechanisms of fRSA may help to counteract rape myths and ensure justice. Our hypothesis of threat-induced involuntary inhibition of voluntary action pathways may contribute to better understanding of the facts about fRSA crimes, the societal wrongs of gender violence and the realities of victims' experience and suffering.

Neuroscientists can also make important contributions to justice in this area. For example, a recent intervention study has shown that educating officers in the neurobiology of voluntary neural mechanisms of fRSA reduced acceptance of rape myths. Moreover, neuroscientifically informed training for officers improved victims' willingness to report rape15. In addition, and in the rape trial context, may improve legal outcomes and justice. Finally, increased awareness of fRSA may benefit victims themselves by reducing victim-blaming, including self-blaming.

A recent Spanish law explicitly requires that consent must be freely and clearly expressed by a person's actions. This progressive and welcome development may help to ensure that the absence of consent could ever be interpreted as consent. Arguments and attitudes that imply that immobility might be misinterpreted as consent are neuroscientifically mistaken. Greater awareness of the realities of fRSA have a role in helping legal systems and wider society to guard against such rape myths.
