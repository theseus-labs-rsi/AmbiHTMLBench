Victims frequently report immobility during rape and sexual assault, often using the term ‘freezing’. Neuroscientific evidence suggests fear and threat can block cortical neural circuits for action control, leading to involuntary immobility. Defence arguments that blame victims for freezing are thus inappropriate and unjust.

Rape and sexual assault (collectively, RSA) are distinctive types of aggressive and criminal human behaviour that are overwhelmingly committed against women. Thirty per cent of women have experienced RSA in their lifetime. The burden of RSA on individuals and whole societies are extensive, but the subject is often hidden. For example, in England and Wales, there were over 70,000 recorded cases of RSA in 2021-2022 but only 3% led to a charge. Neuroscientific contributions to public debate over RSA have been limited.

Consent and rape myths

Legal definitions of RSA are based on the absence of consent. However, victims’ reports of establishing consent or lack thereof is challenging. Victims’ reports of non-consent are often questioned in court. Legal actors in RSA cases are susceptible to stereotypes (referred to here as ‘rape myths’) about how a ‘real’ victim would behave.

One common rape myth implies a perpetrator claiming that he believed the victim was consenting because the victim did not resist: ‘why didn’t she struggle?’ Although struggle and violence play no part in formal legal definitions of RSA in many countries, juries, judges, and wider society, Box 1 gives indicative examples of these misconceptions and how they can transfer the blame for RSA from the perpetrator to the victim.

The ‘why didn’t she struggle?’ argument is based on a cognitive model of action control that underlies all criminal law. Healthy adult humans are assumed to have voluntary control over their actions and to carry out their actions intentionally. Mens rea (conscious intention) and actus reus (action and consequences) are presuppositions. Philosophical and legal arguments hold that one may also be responsible for omitting to act — that not acting may be intentional in the same way that acting can be. In the case of RSA, this argument incorrectly assumes that the victim could have resisted or fought the attacker, yet she intentionally decided not to do so.

The neuroscience of involuntary immobility

Victims may remain immobile because of an involuntary neural mechanism that blocks the brain circuits that provide voluntary control over body movement.

BOX 1 A persistent rape myth

Illustrative quotes from case reports show how defence lawyers and judges misrepresent freezing and immobility during RSA. The term ‘freezing’ is used to describe the victim’s reaction to the assault. Victim testimony is given in double quotation marks; the remaining text reflects court reports of defence lawyer and judgement summaries.

R v Dunsmore (2006) “I just lay there in a way, like I was just scared. And like I didn’t know him and I didn’t know what he could do to me”. (Ref. 18)
R v Lennon (2018) “Did you say anything?” “No.” “Did you do anything?” “No, I just — I didn’t do anything.” “But I suggest, though, that if he was in front of you in the car and he’s forcing that to happen, you could have just simply held your legs together?” “Yes.” “But you didn’t do that?” (Ref. 19)
United States v Townsend (1992) While there was evidence of the victim saying ‘no on several occasions, there was no evidence that further resistance would have been futile. When asked why she froze, [she] answered ‘I don’t know, I was afraid’ and he went on to assault me so — I he just did what he had to do, then he’d — he would just leave’. (Ref. 20)

Further, legal discussions of the involuntary immobility response mention the response as being outside of voluntary control. The law has long recognized loss of motor control defences and can accord diminished responsibility in specific situations in which evidence suggests that actions are made outside of voluntary control. These include some medical conditions such as sleep disorders, but also extreme situations of coercive control, and emotional triggering.
