Inhibitory circuits also exist within the human brainstem itself, but these have not been studied in the context of threat-processing and action control. Here, we draw on the classical concept of motoneurons being suppressed similar to the classical concept of action immobility and a hypnotic "foggy form of immobility". Further research is required to identify and compare the presentation, aetiology and sequelae of these two behaviours, including their implications for victim rehabilitation and for legal outcomes. Importantly, both forms of IRSA would constitute involuntary responses to extreme threat and both would involve suppression of normal voluntary action control.

Involuntary action and the law

Our interpretation of IRSA as an involuntary, threat-induced block of normal voluntary action control raises profound questions about the functioning of specific brain circuits that are centred on the frontal lobes and on the disengagement of other brain circuits that are centred on subcortical structures.

Legal systems posit many concepts for explaining and adjudicating human behaviour. Legal process often poses "why" questions about human behaviour and explains them in terms of reasons. The law distinguishes between actions and omissions whether these are intentional and voluntary, but responsibility is qualified or absent if they are not responsible for actions that are involuntary. Thus, legal concepts of responsibility inevitably implicate the neurobiological basis of the role of cortical voluntary action control in behaviour.

We suggest that given the neural mechanisms that underlie IRSA, the law should not demand that victims explain the omission of voluntary action. As such, legal "why" questions regarding IRSA would not have the same force as for intentional actions or omissions. Legal responses should recognize this fact and ensure it is consistently applied.

We highlight a second issue for legal processing surrounding IRSA. IRSA are freighted with lack of conscious explanatory reasons (box 1). The victim may have difficulty answering "why" questions. Defence lawyers may try to draw attention to a victim's inability to articulate and justify their behaviour during IRSA. This appears to straightforward victim blaming, diverting the attention of the court away from the perpetrator's actions. Victim testimony may be treated as inherently unreliable. In fact, often neuroscientific advances can also help us in understanding why victims often fail to do so. Memories of IRSA are likely to be fragmented, incoherent quality that is characteristic of trauma memories in general.

In addition, memories of IRSA refer to an unprecedented experience – namely, loss of voluntary control over one's own body. Voluntary action is a key aspect of legal personhood. The sudden, unexpected and seldom loss of ability to act in accordance with one's wishes is likely to seem strange and inexplicable even in the absence of trauma. Clinical observations of patients with functional neurological disorder confirms both the disorganization of traumatic memory and the bizarreness of losing

voluntary control" that are also present in IRSA. The law already recognizes in evidential guidelines that trauma may affect the ability to give evidence, but this point seems often to be ignored in legal discussions of IRSA.

Conclusion

We argue that IRSA is an evolutionarily conserved involuntary response that is characterized by lack of normal voluntary motor control, with further distinctive features relating to social avoidance and a lack of subsequent memory effects. We hypothesize that IRSA emerges when the aggressor's behaviours activate the brain threat-defence circuitry of the victim, leading to inhibition of neural pathways that underlie the victim's voluntary action control.

This interaction between neural circuits for threat processing and for voluntary action control may help to explain why individuals show only indirect evidence regarding patterns of neural activity during IRSA. Instead, our argument relies on indirect evidence such as victim testimony and reasoning based on the known neurobiology of this interaction. This evidence, although indirect, is substantial and convergent. Ethical and moral hazards surround almost all research in this area, which is why we have focused on a specific form of action in an attempt to address the legal and social issues raised by IRSA. However, neuroscience is not the only discipline that can inform the law. For example, mechanistic understanding of IRSA may help to counter rape myths and ensure justice. Our hypothesis of threat-induced inhibition of voluntary action may also help in improving understanding of the facts about IRSA, the social wrongs of gender violence, and the realities of victims' experience and behaviour.

Neuroscientists can also make important contributions to justice in this area. For example, a recent intervention study has shown that educating police officers roundelated the involuntary nature of IRSA during legal proceedings, and this training could help to reduce myths of IRSA reduced acceptance of rape myths. Moreover, neuroscientifically informed training for officers improved victims' willingness to engage with legal proceedings and could help to improve legal outcomes and justice. Finally, increased awareness of IRSA may benefit victims themselves by reducing victim-blaming, increasing understanding of the involuntary nature of the experience and reducing the burden of self-blame.

Recent Spanish law explicitly states that consent must be freely and clearly expressed by a person's actions. This progressive development in the law is to be welcomed. However, this could ever be interpreted as consent. Arguments and attitudes that imply that immobility might be misinterpreted as consent are central to neuro-law. We suggest that neuroscientists and wider society must guard against such rape myths.
