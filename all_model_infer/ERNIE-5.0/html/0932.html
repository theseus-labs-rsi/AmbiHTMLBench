Like every creative work, walking for design purposes has to be practiced. Walking designers want to focus on five things. First, learn to open up and intensively perceive the space. Second, allow the slow mode to appear, which means avoiding constant distraction. Third, work on letting the switch between the three modes happen. Fourth, practice getting lost and enjoying the creative process of reorientation. And fifth, rethink transfer first design ideas. Therefore, walking for design should mean walking frequently and learning from more experienced walkers who can assume the role of a trainer (Hannig 2009: 93).

To ensure that all the acquired knowledge and skills can be operative for design processes, professional characteristics, attitudes, and examples have to be put down into images, sketches, and texts. Alice Falsley says that documenting is a way to understand experiences. Unlike texts, she does not try to visualize the walker but instead, documents her emotional response to the walk and the landscape. By transferring this response into drawings, maps, and paintings, Veg Landscape Architects make it accessible for their design process. The perceived atmosphere, styles, processes and structures are available for the landscape design (Fig. 4). 'Some of our perceptions were recorded in field sketches and photographs, and translated in the studio into representative sections, maps, and models. Representing both real and imagined walk I use research focused at Veg, in particular the plan (sketches, aerial photography), and the perspective of the walker (sketches, photographs, film, etc.) (Tveite 2010: 132).'

When walking with inexperienced designers, it is especially important to facilitate an open dialogue in order to be creative. This can mean telling stories or giving precise reports.

Experimenting with walking

Back to our group of landscape architects, urban planners, and geographers in the proximity of Hamburg. On 21 April 2012, they walked some 10 km, in a time frame of about seven hours. They started their walk in Elbeshorst (Landkreis Harburg) and met again in Kirchwerder (Landkreis Stade).

On their search for ideas for the Landscapes of the Hamburger Berge, the Alsen Land, and parts of the river Elbe, they applied a set of rules. The first rule was they avoided looking at their maps and instead they tried to navigate the landscape by following their intuitions and using their three passes. They walked most of the journey alone but tried to talk to people they met on their way, in order to learn more about the landscape. The second rule was they could only shout a maximum of one phrase. Another rule was to find their own, individual rhythm; and a fourth was they immersed themselves into the landscape, trying to enjoy the situation and what they termed, 'play walking'. Before and after the walk, they documented their images and questions that resulted from the walk.
