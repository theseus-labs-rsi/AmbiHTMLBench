Asking for help fosters student success

Asking for help when necessary is critical to success in the workplace, but newcomers often fear how they will be perceived when they request assistance.

Understanding factors that motivate or inhibit various groups of students from seeking help can help us understand how to design interventions that will foster student success.

Most of the time I'm very hesitant to go to their office. I do it, but I'm always scared that they're gonna think I'm an idiot when I walk back out.

Over 90% of women reported using two or more resources compared to less than half the men.

Nearly 30% (n=14) of the students we interviewed stated a strong preference to figure problems out themselves.

This sentiment was more commonly expressed by male students (40%) than female students (25%).

For some of our students, this independence manifested as a source of pride that prevented them from seeking help.

I see most of the students are boys and they are very—I don't know, maybe they get it better than I do. Sometimes I'll feel maybe embarrassed to ask them questions

Positive experiences encourage students
to ask for help

Many students reported feeling more comfortable after a positive experience with an instructor who reached out to them, encouraged them, or expressed a clear passion for teaching.

Students reported developing strategies for asking questions. Most commonly doing as much of the work as possible in advance in order to form specific questions.

I try to do all the work beforehand, so I can point out to him exactly what's going wrong, and why I'm not understanding

Seeking help should be framed as an academic competency

NSF

Students need to be persuaded that when to ask for help and how to ask competently are valued life-long skills.

If students perceive asking for help effectively as an engineering competency, we can engage other marginalized students for asking questions.
