In cats, ventral reticulospinal axons with cell bodies in the gigantocellular nucleus synapse onto inhibitory spinal interneurons, suggesting a candidate mechanism for inhibiting volun- tary action. Inhibitory circuits also exist within the human brainstem itself, but these have not been studied in the context of threat process- ing. Second, we suggest that two forms of IRSA might exist: one in which muscle tone is preserved (similar to the classical concept of tonic immo- bility) and a hypotonic ‘floppy’ form of immobility8. Further research is required to identify and compare the presentation, aetiology and sequelae of these two behaviours, including their implications for victim rehabilitation and for legal outcomes. Importantly, both forms of IRSA would constitute involuntary responses to extreme threat and both would involve the suspension of normal voluntary action control.

Involuntary action and the law

Our interpretation of IRSA as an involuntary, threat-induced block of normal voluntary action control raises profound questions about voluntariness, autonomy and consent. Voluntary action depends on the functioning of specific brain circuits that are centred on the frontal lobes and also on the nonengagement of other brain circuits that are associated with fear and threat, centred on evolutionarily preserved specialized circuits that also involve subcortical structures.

Legal systems provide normative principles for investigating, explaining and judging human voluntary actions13. Legal process often poses ‘why’ questions about human behaviours and explains both actions and omissions by reference to the intentions of agents. Individuals are held responsible for their actions and omissions when these are intentional and voluntary, but responsibility is qualified or reduced when actions and omissions are unintentional – and individu- als are typically not held responsible for actions that are involuntary. Thus, legal concepts of responsibility inevitably implicate the neuro- physiological question of the role of cortical voluntary action circuits in control of behaviour.

We suggest that given the neural mechanisms that underlie responses to severe threat, IRSA can be considered as an involuntary omission of voluntary action. As such, legal ‘why’ questions regard- ing IRSA would not have the same force as for intentional actions or omissions. Legal process should recognize this fact and ensure it is consistently applied.

We highlight a second issue for legal process surrounding IRSA. Legal case reports show that accounts by victims of immobility during RSA are often disjointed and lack conventional explanatory terms (Box 1). The victim may have difficulty answering ‘why’ questions. Defence lawyers often exploit this fact and draw attention to a victim’s inability to articulate and justify their behaviour during RSA. This appears to be the straightforward victim-blaming, diverting the attention of the court away from the aggressive behaviour of the assailant towards the pur- portedly strange behaviour of the victim. In fact, recent neuroscientific advances can also help in understanding why victims often have dif- ficulty in explaining their own behaviour. Victims’ accounts of IRSA are likely to share the fragmented, incoherent quality that is characteristic of traumatic memories in general.

In addition, memories of IRSA refer to an unprecedented experi- ence – namely, losing voluntary control over one’s own body. Voluntary agency is the chief backdrop to mental life in healthy adults, and so a sudden loss of ability to act in accordance with one’s wishes is likely to seem strange and inexplicable even in the absence of trauma. Clinical neuroscientific evidence from a range of conditions confirms both the disorganization of traumatic memory14 and the bizarreness of losing

volitional control15 that are also present in IRSA. The law already rec- ognizes in evidential guidelines that trauma may affect the ability to remember and explain events, including one’s own behaviour – but this point seems often to be ignored in legal discussions of IRSA.

Conclusion

We argue that IRSA is an evolutionarily conserved involuntary response that is characterized by lack of normal voluntary motor control, with further distinctive relations to sexual aggression and fear and with subsequent memory effects. We hypothesize that IRSA emerges when the aggressor’s behaviours activate the brain threat-defence circuitry of the victim, leading to the inhibition of neural pathways that underlie the victim’s voluntary action control.

This interaction between neural circuits for threat processing and for voluntary action remains a scientific hypothesis, as we can have only indirect evidence regarding patterns of neural activity during IRSA. Instead, our argument relies on indirect evidence such as victim testimony and evidence from studies of defence circuitry in animals. This evidence, although indirect, is substantial and convergent. Ethi- cal and moral hazards surround almost all research in this area, which makes it difficult for neuroscientific research to directly address the legal and societal issues raised by RSA. However, neuroscience should still, in our view, contribute to public debate regarding RSA. For example, mechanistic understanding of IRSA may help to counter- act rape myths and ensure justice. Our hypothesis of threat-induced involuntary inhibition of voluntary action pathways may contribute to improving understanding of the facts about RSA crimes, the societal wrongs of gender violence, and the realities of victims’ experience and suffering.

Neuroscientists can also make important contributions to justice in this area. For example, a recent intervention study has shown that educating police officers to understand the involuntary neural mecha- nisms of IRSA reduced acceptance of rape myths. Moreover, neurosci- entifically informed training for officers improved victims’ willingness to continue with legal proceedings16 and such training could potentially improve legal outcomes and justice. Finally, increased awareness of IRSA may benefit victims themselves by reducing victim-blaming, including self-blaming and inappropriate feelings of guilt.

A recent Spanish law explicitly requires that consent must be freely and clearly expressed by a person’s actions. This progressive and enlightened legislation clearly rules out the rape myth that IRSA could ever be interpreted as consent. Arguments and attitudes that imply that immobility might be misinterpreted as consent are neuro- scientifically mistaken, and unjustly blame victims. Neuroscience may have a role in helping legal systems and wider society to guard against such rape myths.
