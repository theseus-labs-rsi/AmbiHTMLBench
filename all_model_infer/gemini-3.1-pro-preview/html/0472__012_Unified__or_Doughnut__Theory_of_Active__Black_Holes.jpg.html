Anatomy of an Active Black Hole

[Image: Supermassive Black Hole]
Supermassive Black Hole
Size: as big as Mercury's orbit
[Image: "Doughnut" Ring of Dust & Gas]
"Doughnut" Ring of Dust & Gas
Size: around 1 light-year
[Image: Host Galaxy]
Host Galaxy
Size: about 100,000 light-years

Astronomers call supermassive black
holes and their surrounding disks
"Active Galactic Nuclei" or "AGN"

Black holes emit no light, but
the glow from the bright
material swirling around them
can be seen by telescopes.

Why Do Active Black Holes Look Different?

[Image: Face-on view of black hole]

In some galaxies the
black hole regions are
incredibly bright, while in
other galaxies they
appear much darker.

A "unified model"
suggests the angle at
which we view them can
explain the differences.

[Image: Edge-on view of black hole]

Seen face-on, we detect the light from the
hot material surrounding the black hole.

When viewed through the edge of the dusty
doughnut, the black hole is hidden.

What We Expect to See

[Image: Scattered galaxies in various orientations]

Galaxies are oriented randomly in the sky so the disks
in their centers should be oriented randomly as well.

Thus we expect to see a random mix of exposed and
hidden black holes everywhere we look.

WISE Observations Challenge "Unified Model"

[Image: WISE Satellite]

Observations with NASA's Wide-
field Infrared Survey Explorer
reveal that hidden black holes
tend to be more clumped
together than exposed ones.

This violates the "unified model,"
which predicts we should see
the same random distribution of
dusty doughnuts regardless of
how clumped the objects are.

[Image: Strongly clumped galaxies shown in purple]

Strongly clumped galaxies are likely to have larger halos of dark matter (shown
artistically here in purple), which might provide a clue about what else besides the
doughnuts could be hiding the black holes.
