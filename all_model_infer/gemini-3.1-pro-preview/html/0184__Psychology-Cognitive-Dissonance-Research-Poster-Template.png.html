Performing

Study conducted by Jonas Festinger,
Marcus Carlsmith & Gabriel Chessman
[Graphic: Three white head silhouettes containing a gear, an exclamation mark, and a question mark]
REFERENCES:
Walsh, Karen., Kemputo, Odelle., "The psychology of self-deception"
The Anaptic Journal, 2017 • Satuda, Evelyn., Temitope, Anya., "Neuroscience
of Decision-Making" The Science Review, 2018 • Brahms, Hansen., Leigh,
Anderson., "The Need and Neurology for Happiness", The Arch Journal, 2020
Purpose:
Festinger, Carlsmith and Chessman wanted to understand if making people perform a menial task would result in cognitive dissonance as a result of forced compliance behavior.
[Icon: Cyan gears and people silhouettes]
Hypothesis:
When asked to do an unenjoyable task, people will not readily admit as much, and tell themselves and others it is actually enjoyable.
[Icon: White thumbs up and balance scale]
Participants:
In a lab setting, 71 male students as participants to perform a series of menial tasks (like turning pegs in a peg board for an hour).
[Icon: White clock and pegboard]
Method:
The participants were then paid either $1 or $20 to tell a waiting participant (a test confederate) that the tasks were really engaging and fun. Almost all of the participants agreed to walk into the waiting room and persuade the confederate that the boring experiment would be interesting.
[Icon: Cyan speech bubble and hand holding money]
Results:
When the participants were asked to evaluate the experiment, the participants who were paid only $1 rated the tedious task as more fun and enjoyable than the participants who were paid $20 to lie.
[Icon: Cyan upward arrow and star with plus sign]
Conclusion:
Getting paid $1 is not sufficient for lying, so those who were paid $1 experienced dissonance. These participants could only overcome that dissonance by coming to believe that the tasks really were interesting and enjoyable.

Being paid $20 provides a reason, therefore there is no dissonance.
[Icon: White hand stamping and head with gear]
