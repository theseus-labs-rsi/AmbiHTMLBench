Probability Theory

Probability theory is the branch of mathematics concerned with the analysis of random phenomena. The central objects of probability theory are random variables, stochastic processes, and events: mathematical abstractions of non-deterministic events or measured quantities that may either be single occurrences or evolve over time in an apparently random fashion.

The foundation of probability theory is built upon the three axioms of probability, often referred to as the Kolmogorov axioms. Let \( \Omega \) be a sample space, \( \mathcal{F} \) a \(\sigma\)-algebra of subsets of \( \Omega \), and \( P \) a probability measure on \( (\Omega, \mathcal{F}) \). Then:

$$ \begin{aligned} &(1)\quad P(A) \ge 0 \quad \text{for all } A \in \mathcal{F},\\[4pt] &(2)\quad P(\Omega) = 1,\\[4pt] &(3)\quad \text{If } A_1, A_2, \dots \text{ are pairwise disjoint, then } P\!\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty} A_i\right) = \sum_{i=1}^{\infty} P(A_i). \end{aligned} $$

From these axioms, many useful properties follow, such as the complement rule \( P(A^c) = 1 - P(A) \), the inclusion–exclusion principle, and the monotonicity of probability.

Conditional Probability and Independence

For two events \( A \) and \( B \) with \( P(B) > 0 \), the conditional probability of \( A \) given \( B \) is defined as

$$ P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}. $$

This leads to the law of total probability and Bayes' theorem:

$$ P(B) = \sum_{i} P(B \mid A_i)\,P(A_i), \qquad P(A_j \mid B) = \frac{P(B \mid A_j)\,P(A_j)}{P(B)}. $$

Two events \( A \) and \( B \) are said to be independent if

$$ P(A \cap B) = P(A)\,P(B). $$

Random Variables

A random variable \( X \) is a measurable function from a sample space \( \Omega \) to a measurable space \( \mathcal{X} \) (typically \( \mathbb{R} \)). The distribution of \( X \) is characterized by its cumulative distribution function (CDF)

$$ F_X(x) = P(X \le x), \qquad x \in \mathbb{R}. $$

For a discrete random variable, the probability mass function (PMF) is \( p_X(x) = P(X = x) \). For a continuous random variable, the probability density function (PDF) \( f_X(x) \) satisfies

$$ F_X(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{x} f_X(t)\,dt, \qquad \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f_X(t)\,dt = 1. $$
Distribution Type Parameters PMF / PDF
Binomial Discrete \( n \in \mathbb{N},\; p \in [0,1] \) \( \displaystyle \binom{n}{k} p^k (1-p)^{n-k} \)
Poisson Discrete \( \lambda > 0 \) \( \displaystyle e^{-\lambda} \frac{\lambda^k}{k!} \)
Normal (Gaussian) Continuous \( \mu \in \mathbb{R},\; \sigma^2 > 0 \) \( \displaystyle \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} e^{-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}} \)
Exponential Continuous \( \lambda > 0 \) \( \lambda e^{-\lambda x},\; x \ge 0 \)

Expectation and Variance

The expected value (or mean) of a random variable \( X \) is defined as

$$ \mathbb{E}[X] = \begin{cases} \displaystyle \sum_{x} x\,p_X(x), & X \text{ discrete},\\[10pt] \displaystyle \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x\,f_X(x)\,dx, & X \text{ continuous}. \end{cases} $$

The variance measures the spread of the distribution:

$$ \operatorname{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}\!\left[(X - \mathbb{E}[X])^2\right] = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - (\mathbb{E}[X])^2. $$

The standard deviation is \( \sigma_X = \sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X)} \). For any constants \( a, b \in \mathbb{R} \), we have

$$ \mathbb{E}[aX + b] = a\,\mathbb{E}[X] + b, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(aX + b) = a^2 \operatorname{Var}(X). $$

Law of Large Numbers and Central Limit Theorem

Let \( X_1, X_2, \dots \) be i.i.d. random variables with finite mean \( \mu \) and variance \( \sigma^2 \). The weak law of large numbers states that the sample average converges in probability to the population mean:

$$ \overline{X}_n = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} X_i \;\xrightarrow{p}\; \mu. $$

The central limit theorem (CLT) describes the asymptotic distribution of the standardized sample mean:

$$ \frac{\overline{X}_n - \mu}{\sigma / \sqrt{n}} \;\xrightarrow{d}\; \mathcal{N}(0,1). $$

This result is fundamental to statistical inference, as it justifies the use of the normal distribution for large-sample approximations.

Definition 1.1 (σ-algebra). A collection \( \mathcal{F} \) of subsets of \( \Omega \) is called a \(\sigma\)-algebra if it satisfies: (i) \( \Omega \in \mathcal{F} \); (ii) if \( A \in \mathcal{F} \) then \( A^c \in \mathcal{F} \); (iii) if \( A_1, A_2, \dots \in \mathcal{F} \) then \( \bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty} A_i \in \mathcal{F} \).

Notes and Further Reading

The material presented in this chapter provides a concise overview of the core concepts of probability theory. For a more rigorous treatment, see Probability and Measure by Billingsley, or Probability: Theory and Examples by Durrett. The axiomatic framework due to Kolmogorov remains the standard foundation for modern probability.
