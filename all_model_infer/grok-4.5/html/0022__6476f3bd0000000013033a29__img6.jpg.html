CAPITALIZATION AND PUNCTUATION

This section covers the fundamental rules of capitalization and punctuation used in the English language.

1. Capitalization

1.1 Proper Nouns

The names of specific people, places, and things are capitalized:

• Abraham Lincoln

• Mount Everest

• Microsoft Corporation

• Paris

1.2 The First Word of a Sentence

Every sentence begins with a capital letter:

• The quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog.

1.3 Days, Months, and Holidays

Capitalize the names of days, months, and holidays:

• Monday, July 4th, Christmas Day

1.4 Titles and Honorifics

Capitalize titles when they precede a person’s name:

• Dr. Martin Luther King Jr.

• President Joe Biden

• Senator Kamala Harris

2. Punctuation

2.1 Periods (Full Stops)

Used at the end of declarative sentences and after abbreviations:

• The meeting is at 3:00 p.m.

2.2 Question Marks

Used at the end of interrogative sentences:

• Is that the truth?

2.3 Exclamation Points

Used at the end of exclamatory sentences:

• What a beautiful day!

2.4 Commas

Used to separate items in a list, independent clauses, and nonessential elements:

• I like apples, oranges, and bananas.

• When you arrive, we will begin.

2.5 Semicolons

Used to join closely related independent clauses:

• I love to read; you prefer television.

2.6 Colons

Used to introduce a list or explanation:

• Bring the following items: pencils, paper, and erasers.

2.7 Apostrophes

Used for contractions and possession:

• It’s a beautiful day. (contraction)

• The dog’s tail is wagging. (possession)

2.8 Quotation Marks

Used to enclose direct quotations:

• She said, “I love you.”

2.9 Dashes and Parentheses

Used for emphasis and asides:

• The meeting will be held—please be on time.

Common Punctuation Errors to Avoid

Never use a comma before a coordinating conjunction in a list.

Do not capitalize “I” in the middle of a sentence.

3. Special Cases

Numbers and symbols follow specific rules:

• Dates: January 1, 2026

• Addresses: 123 Main Street, Anytown, USA 12345
