In cats, ventral reticulospinal axons with cell bodies in the gigantocellular nucleus synapse onto inhibitory spinal interneurons12, suggesting a second brain mechanism for inhibiting voluntary action. Inhibitory circuits also exist within the human brainstem itself, but these have not been studied in the context of threat processing. Second, we suggest that two forms of IRSA might exist: one where muscle tone is preserved (similar to the classical concept of tonic immobility) and a hypotonic 'floppy' form of immobility7. Further research is required to investigate how these mechanisms relate to the cause and sequelae of these two behaviours, including their implications for victim rehabilitation and for legal outcomes. Importantly, both forms of IRSA are likely to be involuntary because the neural pathways for both would involve the suspension of normal voluntary action control.

Involuntary action and the law

Our interpretation of IRSA as an involuntary, threat-induced block of normal voluntary action control raises profound questions about criminal responsibility. Currently, most legal systems depend on the functioning of specific brain circuits that are centred on the frontal lobes and exclude the engagement of other brain circuits that are associated with fear and the stress response13. Legal responsibility is a public policy instrument for investigating, explaining and judging human voluntary actions14. Legal process often poses 'why' questions about human behaviours and explains them by attributing intentions, motives and states of mind to actors. Individuals are held responsible for their actions and omissions when these are intentional and voluntary, but responsibility is qualified or excused when they are not. For example, children and individuals who are typically not held responsible for actions that are involuntary. Thus, legal concepts of responsibility inevitably implicate the neurophysiological processes of the role of cortical voluntary action circuits in control of behaviour.

We suggest that given the neural mechanisms that underlie involuntary action control, IRSA can be considered as an involuntary omission of voluntary action. As such, legal 'why' questions regarding IRSA would not have the same force as for intentional actions or omissions. Legal process should recognize this fact and ensure it is consistently applied.

We highlight a second issue for legal process surrounding IRSA. Legal process assumes that victims of crimes against bodily integrity are often disjointed and lack conventional explanatory terms (Box 1). The victim may have difficulty answering 'why' questions. Defence lawyers and judges may pay undue attention to inconsistencies in order to articulate and justify their behaviour during RSA. This appears to be strategic toward victim blame, diverting the attention of the court away from the aggressive behaviour of the assailant towards the purportedly strange behaviour of the victim. In fact, recent neuroscientific studies show that victims understand why victims often have difficulty in explaining their own behaviour. Victim accounts of IRSA are likely to share the fragmented, incoherent quality that is characteristic of traumatic memories.

In addition, memories of IRSA refer to an unprecedented experience — namely, losing voluntary control over one's own body. Voluntary agency is the chief backdrop to mental life in healthy adults, and the sudden loss of ability to act in accordance with one's wishes is likely to seem strange and inexplicable even in the absence of trauma. Conclusion

We argue that IRSA is an evolutionarily conserved involuntary response that is characterized by lack of normal voluntary motor control, with further distinctive relations to sexual aggression, dissociation and with subsequent memory effects. We hypothesize that IRSA emerges when the aggressor's behaviours activate the brain threat-defence circuitry and that this activation disrupts the neural pathways that underlie the victim's voluntary action control.

This interaction between neural circuits for threat processing and for voluntary action remains speculative at this stage because we have only indirect evidence regarding patterns of neural activity during IRSA. Instead, our argument relies on indirect evidence such as victim reports and behavioural experiments that provide converging support. This evidence, although indirect, is substantial and convergent. Ethical and methodological surround almost all research on IRSA makes it extremely difficult for the cognitive neuroscientist to directly address the legal and societal issues raised by IRSA. However, neuroscience should still, in our view, contribute to the debate regarding RSA. For example, mechanistic understanding of IRSA may help to counteract rape myths and ensure justice. Our hypothesis of threat-induced suppression of voluntary action implies that neuroscience may improve understanding of the facts about RSA crimes, the societal wrongs of gender violence, and the realities of victims' experience and suffering.

Neuroscientists can also make important contributions to justice in this area. For example, a recent intervention study has shown that educating police officers to understand the involuntary neural mechanisms of IRSA reduced acceptance of rape myths. Moreover, neuroscientifically informed training for officers improved victims' willingness to continue with legal proceedings18, and such training could potentially improve legal outcomes and justice. Finally, increased awareness of IRSA may benefit victims themselves by reducing victim-blaming, increasing self-understanding and facilitating recovery.

A recent Spanish law explicitly requires that consent must be freely and clearly expressed by a person's actions. This progressive change in law acknowledges the possibility that the victim's behaviour could ever be interpreted as consent. Arguments and attitudes that imply that immobility might be misinterpreted as consent are neuroscientifically uninformed and profoundly blame victims. Neuroscience may have a role in helping legal systems and wider society to guard against such rape myths.
