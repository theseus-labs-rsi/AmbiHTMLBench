Neuroscience evidence counters a rape myth

Ebani Dhawan & Patrick Haggard Check for updates
Victims frequently report immobility during rape and sexual assault, often using the term 'freezing'. Neuroscientific evidence suggests fear and threat can block cortical neural circuits for action control, leading to involuntary immobility. Defence arguments that blame victims for freezing are thus inappropriate and unjust.

Rape and sexual assault (collectively, RSA) are distinctive types of aggressive and criminal human behaviour that are overwhelmingly directed against women and girls. Thirty per cent of women have experienced RSA in their lifetime. The burdens of RSA on individuals and societies are enormous — there were more than 68,000 reports for example, in England and Wales the police recorded over 70,000 rapes in 2021–2022 but only 3% led to a charge. Neuroscientific contributions to public debate over RSA have been limited.

Consent and rape myths

Legal definitions of RSA are based on the absence of consent. However, establishing consent or lack thereof is challenging. Victims' reports of non-consent are often questioned in court. Legal actors in RSA cases sometimes use stereotypes (often referred to here as 'rape myths') about how a 'real' victim would behave.

One common rape myth1 involves a perpetrator claiming that he had clear permission from the alleged victim. He then attempts to shift responsibility by asking: why didn't she struggle? Although struggle and violence play no part in current legal definitions of RSA, the law has historically used such considerations to influence the thinking of jurors, lawyers and judges, and of wider society. Box 1 gives indicative examples of these myths, showing how courts seek to transfer the blame for RSA from the perpetrator to the victim.

The 'why didn't she struggle?' argument is based on a cognitive model of voluntary action that underlies all criminal law. Healthy adult humans are assumed to have voluntary control over their actions and to carry out their actions intentionally. Mens rea (conscious intention) makes an important contribution to moral judgement and consequences. Philosophical and legal arguments hold that one may also be responsible for omitting to act — that not acting may be intentional in the same way that acting is intentional. In either case of RSA, this argument incorrectly assumes that the victim could have resisted or fled the attacker, yet she intentionally decided not to do so.

The neuroscience of involuntary immobility

We propose a new, neuroscientific and empirically testable hypothesis: immobility over resistance is neuroscientifically incorrect. We suggest that RSA victims may remain immobile because of an involuntary neural response to threat that blocks the brain circuits that provide voluntary control over body movement.

Further, legal discussions of the involuntary immobility response may involve an inappropriate double standard. The law has long recognized 'loss of control' defences and can accord diminished responsibility in specific situations in which evidence suggests that actions are made outside of voluntary control. These include some medical conditions such as sleep disorders, but also extreme situations of coercive control, and emotional triggering.

In contrast, few legal systems should likewise recognize that the omission of action may also sometimes be involuntary. A involuntary immobility response may prevent a victim of RSA from responding by escaping or deterring the aggressor. This does not imply that the victim ought to make such actions; obligations and responsibilities should lie with the aggressor, and not with the victim. Improved legal understanding of neuroscientific evidence regarding involuntary immobility during RSA could prevent inappropriate victim-blaming, and help to confer wider societal attention to the crucial importance of active consent. Clearly, it is also possible to be voluntarily immobile without actively consenting, but our aim here is to draw attention to the frequency, mechanism and legal implications of involuntary immobility.
