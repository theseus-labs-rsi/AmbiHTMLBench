context-appropriate action22. In cats, ventral reticulospinal axons with collaterals in the gigantocellular nucleus synapse onto inhibitory spinal interneurons, suggesting a candidate mechanism for inhibiting voluntary action. Inhibitory circuits also exist within the human brainstem itself, but these have not been studied in the context of threat processing. Second, we suggest that two forms of RSA might exist: one in which muscle tone is preserved (similar to the classical concept of tonic immobility) and a hypotonic ‘floppy’ form of immobility7. Further research is required to identify and compare the presentation, aetiology and sequelae of these two behaviours, including their implications for victim rehabilitation and for legal outcomes. Importantly, both forms of RSA would constitute involuntary responses to extreme threat and both would involve the suspension of normal voluntary action control.

Involuntary action and the law

Our interpretation of RSA as an involuntary, threat-induced block of normal voluntary action control raises profound questions about voluntariness, autonomy and consent. Voluntary action depends on the functioning of specific brain circuits that are centred on the frontal lobes and also on the nonengagement of other brain circuits that are associated with fear and threat, control the evolutionarily preserved specialized circuits that also involve brain cortical structures.

Legal systems provide normative principles for investigating, explaining and judging human voluntary actions1. Legal process often poses ‘why’ questions about human behaviours and explains both actions and omissions by reference to the intentions of agents. Individuals are held responsible for their actions and omissions when these are intentional and voluntary, but responsibility is qualified or reduced when actions and omissions are unintentional – and individual acts typically not held responsible for actions that are involuntary. Thus, legal concepts of responsibility inevitably implicate the neurobiological question of whether a cortically voluntary action circuit was in control of behaviour.

We suggest that given the neural mechanisms that underlie responses to severe threat, RSA is best considered as an involuntary omission of voluntary action. As such, legal ‘why’ questions regarding RSA would not have the same force as for intentional actions or omissions. Legal process should recognize this fact and ensure it is consistently applied.

We highlight a second issue for legal process surrounding RSA. Legal processes show that accounts by victims of immobility during RSA are often disjointed and lack conventional explanatory terms (Box 1). The victim may have difficulty answering ‘why’ questions. Defence lawyers often exploit this fact and draw attention to a victim’s inability to articulate and justify their behaviour during RSA. This appears to be straightforward victim-blaming, diverting the attention of the court away from the aggressive behaviour of the assailant towards the purportedly strange behaviour of the victim. In fact, recent neuroscientific advances can also help in understanding why victims often have difficulty in explaining their own behaviour. Victims’ accounts of RSA are likely to share the fragmentary, sequential quality that is characteristic of traumatic memories in general.

In addition, victims of RSA report a fragmented experienced experience – namely, losing voluntary control over one’s own body. Voluntary agency is the chief backdrop to mental life in healthy adults, and so a sudden loss of ability to control in accordance with one’s wishes is likely to seem strange and inexplicable even to the person of a trauma. Clinical neuroscientific evidence from a range of conditions confirms that the disorganization of a traumatic memory23 and the bizarreness of losing

volitional control14 that are also present in RSA. The law already recognizes in evidential guidelines that trauma may affect the ability to remember and explain events, including one’s own behaviour – but this point seems often to be ignored in legal discussions of RSA.

Conclusion

We argue that RSA is an evolutionarily conserved involuntary response that is characterized by lack of normal voluntary motor control, with further distinctive relations to sexual aggression and fear and with subsequent memory effects. We hypothesize that RSA emerges when the aggressor’s behaviours activate the brain threat-defence circuitry of the victim, leading to the inhibition of the pathways that underlie the victim’s voluntary action control.

This integrated framework clarifies circuits for threat processing and for voluntary action remains a scientific hypothesis, as we can have only indirect evidence regarding patterns of neural activity during RSA. Instead, our argument relies on indirect evidence such as victim testimony and evidence from studies of defence circuitry in animals. This evidence, although indirect, is substantial and convergent. Ethical and moral hazards surround almost all research in this area, which makes it difficult for neuroscientific research to directly address the legal and societal issues raised by RSA. However, neuroscience should still, in our view, contribute to public debate regarding RSA. For example, mechanistic understanding of RSA may help to counteract rape myths and ensure justice. Our hypothesis of threat-induced and automatic inhibition of voluntary action pathways may contribute to improving understanding of the facts about RSA crimes, the societal wrongs of gender violence, and the realities of victims’ experience and suffering.

Neuroscientists can also contribute to important questions of justice in this area. For example, a recent intervention study has shown that educating police officers to understand the involuntary neural mechanisms of RSA reduced acceptance of rape myths. Moreover, neuroscientifically informed training for officers improved victims’ willingness to continue with legal proceedings24 and such training could potentially improve legal outcomes and justice. Finally, increased awareness of RSA may benefit victims themselves by reducing victim-blaming, including self-blaming and inappropriate feelings of guilt.

A recent Spanish law explicitly requires that consent must be freely and clearly expressed by a person’s actions. This progressive and enlightened legislation clearly rules out the rape myth that RSA could ever be interpreted as consent. Arguments and attitudes that imply that immobility might be misinterpreted as consent are neuroscientifically mistaken, and unjustly blame victims. Neuroscience may have a role in helping legal systems and wider society to guard against such myths.
