Like every creative work, walking for design purposes has to be practiced. Walking designers went out to focus on five things. First, learn to open up and intensively perceive the space. Second, allow the flow mode to appear, which means avoiding conscious reflection. Third, work on letting the switch between the three modes happen. Fourth, practice getting lost and enjoying the creative process of reorientation. And fifth, rehearse transferring first ideas into designs. Therefore, walking for design should mean practising with or learning from more experienced walkers who can assume the role of a trainer (Ingold 2007: 93).

To ensure that all described knowledge and ideas can be creative for design processes, the experienced characteristics, atmospheres, and ensembles have to be put down in images, sketches, and texts. Also Foxley says that documenting is a way to translate experiences. Unlike Lukas, she does not try to visualize the landscape but instead, documents her emotional response to the walk and the landscape. By transferring this response into drawings, maps, and paintings, Vogt Landscape Architects make it accessible for their design process. The perceived atmosphere, stories, processes, and structures are available as the basis for each design (Fig. 4). ‘Some of our perceptions were recorded in field sketches and photographs, and translated in the studio into representative sections, maps, and models. Representing both real and imagined walks is one research focus at Vogt, in particular the plan view (maps, aerial photographs, etc.) and the perspective of the walker (sketches, photographs, film, etc.) (Foxley 2010: 173).’

When walking with inexperienced designers, it is especially important to facilitate an open dialogue in order to be creative. This can mean telling stories rather than giving precise reports.

Experimenting with walking

Back to our group of landscape architects, urban planners, and geographers in the periphery of Hamburg. On 21 April 2012, they walked some 20 km, in a time frame of about seven hours. They started their walk in Eberstorf (Landkreis Harburg) and met again in Königreich (Landkreis Stade).

On their search for ideas for the landscapes of the Harburger Berge, the Altes Land, and parts of the river Elbe, they applied a set of rules. The first rule was the avoidance of looking at their maps; instead they tried to navigate the landscape by following their intuition and using their compasses. They walked most of the day on their own and tried to talk to people they met on their route to learn more about the landscapes. The second rule was [illegible]. Another rule was to find their own, individual rhythm; and a fourth was that they immersed themselves into the landscapes, trying to enjoy the excursion and what they termed, ‘play walking’. Before and after the walk, they documented their images and questions that resulted from the walk.
